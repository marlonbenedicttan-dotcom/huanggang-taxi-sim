What should be done first?

Fix the queue ticket. Average wait falls from 20 minutes to 9.

  1. Fix the ticket first. Phone or number-plate check-in instead of a paper ticket at the buffer. Drivers stop losing eight minutes a trip, so more of them come. Software money, about HK$2m — and the trade is already asking for it.
  2. Then push for a cross-boundary quota. Licensed taxis carry someone in and someone out, so the drive to the port is paid instead of empty. The biggest win, but it needs an agreement with Shenzhen.
  3. Only grow the pre-booked lane if you build stands for it. With two stands, sending more people there makes the queue longer, not shorter.

How the simulation works

In one sentence: it runs a whole day one minute at a time — people arrive and queue for a taxi, empty taxis arrive and wait, and whoever pairs up first leaves.

The port as it is today

The rebuilt Huanggang Port opened on 12 October 2026 and runs 24 hours. Its ground-floor public transport interchange has separate red (urban) and green (New Territories) taxi stands, plus a dedicated area and lounge for pre-booked licensed fleet taxis. No other ride-hailing pickup is allowed inside the boundary control area.

Every empty taxi — and every pre-booked fleet taxi — must first collect a paper queue ticket at a public transport buffer zone, then wait to be called forward to the stand, and hand the ticket back on the way in. The taxi trade reports that this costs about eight minutes a trip, paid by the driver rather than the passenger, and has asked the Transport Department to review it. The trade has also asked for real-time passenger numbers so drivers can judge whether a trip to the port is worth it.

That ticket time is the single most important thing in this model: it is the difference between a taxi choosing to come here or staying in the city.

Minute by minute

  1. People arrive at the terminal doors at the rate you set (550 an hour by default), following the real shape of a day — quiet morning, busy peaks. Each one is randomly urban (red), New Territories (green) or pre-booked (blue).
  2. They join their lane's queue. Everyone starts at the back and shuffles forward. They also have a patience limit: wait longer than 35 minutes and they leave — that is the "gave up" number.
  3. Empty taxis arrive from the city at the rate set by the supply sliders (1.05× demand for urban, 0.95× for NT). They park in the buffer at the entrance.
  4. Staff release taxis from the buffer into the pickup bays — in a batch every minute in the current process, or one at a time with continuous dispatch.
  5. A taxi takes the next person in its lane and boards them. Boarding takes about 1.5 minutes, or 25% less if they pre-registered on the app.
  6. The taxi leaves with the passenger and the next taxi takes the bay.
  7. If the buffer is full, arriving taxis circle and come back later — so a full buffer quietly costs you supply.
  8. If a taxi is sent to the wrong lane, it has to circle back. That is the misrouting setting, and it wastes time for everyone.
  9. Repeat for 12 hours, then report the averages.

What the numbers mean

  • Mean wait — average time from arriving at the hall to sitting in a taxi, counting everyone who arrived: people who gave up are counted up to the moment they left. Counting only the people who were served would flatter any policy that pushes the longest waiters out of the statistics. (The research tables in the README report the served-only mean, which is always lower.)
  • 90th percentile — the unlucky one in ten. The tail is what people complain about, and it is always worse than the average.
  • Gave up — share who left before getting a taxi. The policy cards are ranked on a mean that includes these people, precisely so a policy cannot look good by pushing the longest waiters out of the statistics.
  • Avg waiting — how many people are in the queues at any moment.
  • Avg bays busy — share of the time each bay is occupied. Near 100% means the bays, not the taxis, are the limit.
  • Avg taxis waiting — empty taxis in the buffer. High, while people still wait? The problem is bays or dispatch, not supply.

Why it behaves the way it does

The bays are the bottleneck. Eight urban bays boarding one taxi every 1.5 minutes can serve about 320 parties an hour, and the urban lane receives about 317 — the two are almost identical, which is why the queue builds at the peak and why adding bays helps so much.

Adding taxis only helps while taxis are the constraint. Once the buffer is full, more supply changes nothing. And demand hurts more than proportionally, because empty taxis keep arriving at roughly the same rate whether the hall is busy or not — that is what makes a demand surge bite.

Driver incentives — the return leg

A taxi that brings someone to the port has no guaranteed fare back, so the trip is worth less than an hour in the city and many drivers simply skip it. The three incentive policies raise what the trip is worth and therefore how many taxis turn up.

The size of that response is assumed, not measured — nobody has published a driver supply elasticity for this port. The shape is what to trust: the benefit saturates (matching a fifth of return trips captures most of it) and the instruments substitute for each other rather than adding up.

Policies that were tested and left out

Not everything that helps in the abstract fits this port. These were simulated, then dropped because they either do nothing here or would contradict the way Huanggang actually operates:

  • Guaranteed return fare — the walk-up rank is first-come-first-served by ticket order; a pre-matched fare conflicts with that. Pre-booking already exists in the fleet channel, which is why the app card is "fleet pre-registration".
  • Port pickup surcharge — Hong Kong taxi fares are uniform by meter and there is no location surcharge anywhere; this would need a fare amendment, and nobody is proposing one.
  • Flexible taxis — green taxis may reach the port, but they remain licensed for New Territories destinations. Letting them take urban fares contradicts the zoning, so the model would promise something the law forbids.
  • Pooled pickup bays — the interchange has separate red and green stands by licence; pooling them is not an operational choice.
  • Continuous dispatch and digital lane routing — both measured at about zero (−0.05 and −0.02 min). The dispatch cycle and misrouting are not the constraint.

Still open: the trade's request for real-time passenger information. The model cannot answer it yet, and the answer is not obvious — telling drivers the queue is long may keep them away exactly when passengers need them.

What it does not model

  • Walking and waiting inside the terminal, and the border process itself.
  • Weather, accidents, roadworks, or a closed lane.
  • Driver shifts, rest breaks and shift changes.
  • Competition with buses, the MTR or cross-border coaches.
